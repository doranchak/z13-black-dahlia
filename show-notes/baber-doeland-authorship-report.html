Is “Jack Doeland” Alex Baber?

Authorship analysis of the YouTube account @JackDoeland against the Alex Baber corpus · prepared for David Oranchak · Oct 14, 2026 · local file, not published

Bottom line. Several independent kinds of evidence point the same way. Four of them are hard to explain if Doeland were an unrelated fan:

  • An invisible-character typing glitch inside @-handles that, among 2,748 accounts, appears only for @CCCOA.US_2021 and @JackDoeland.
  • Baber’s malapropism “distractors”, used by Doeland when the only written instance was Baber’s private message to David.
  • Knowledge of “Trevor”, an alias that appears elsewhere only in Baber’s private email.
  • A Fridland-style habit profile that matches Baber on 21 of 24 distinctive habits, when no other commenter exceeds 12.

Machine-learning and stylometric tests point the same way, but with smaller margins. Taken together, the evidence strongly supports common authorship, or at minimum Baber’s direct hand in the account (for example, someone posting with him, on his device). It is an investigative finding, not forensic certainty. See §4 for the differences that cut the other way.

4StrongFridland methodStylometry

Fridland-style habit scorecard: Doeland shares 21 of Baber’s 24 distinctive habits; no one else shares more than 12

Following Fridland’s scorecard approach (she compared 28 categories and found Zodiac and Margolis shared 21), 42 measurable habits were defined across her categories: punctuation, spelling/grammar errors, sentence structure, rhetorical moves, modal verbs and word choice. Each account was measured on equal-size 2,000-word samples. A habit “counts” only if the account uses it more than three-quarters of the 21 background commenters do. That background comparison is the step Fridland’s analysis lacked. Baber’s YouTube writing shows 24 such habits. Doeland shares 21. The best of the 21 heavy background commenters shares 12, and the median is 4. Against Baber’s other written work (Facebook, emails, Substack): Doeland 23 of 27; best background account 15.

Bars: number of Baber’s distinctive habits each account also shows. Red: Doeland. Amber: the other suspected accounts. The full habit table is in §3.

Baseline: Doeland is 9 habits above the best background account (Cragle-Z, 12).

Caveat: The habits are not independent; some come in clusters, such as em-dash habits. Rates also differ: Doeland uses em dashes about a quarter as often as Baber. A Delta-style distance over the raw habit rates does not single Doeland out (§5).

7ModerateStylometry

Topic-masked character n-grams: Doeland’s four nearest writers are all Baber

Content words were masked (Stamatatos “text distortion”) so that topic doesn’t drive the comparison, leaving punctuation, function words and word shapes. Doeland’s full text was then compared with 26 profiles: 21 heavy commenters plus 5 Baber registers (YouTube; Facebook/X/Instagram; letters and messages; Substack and website; speech). Doeland’s nearest four are Baber’s four written registers (BABER_YT, BABER_CORR, BABER_SOCIAL, BABER_PUB), then @TM-ew1ig.

Baseline: None of the 21 background accounts has Baber registers in all four nearest places; the most any has is 3.

Caveat: Baber’s large, varied corpus makes his profiles “central”, so many accounts have one Baber register near the top. Only the clean sweep is unusual.

8ModerateStylometryMachine learning

General Impostors verification: Doeland vs Baber scores 0.53. No background account scores above 0.34 against Baber.

The General Impostors method (Koppel & Winter 2014) asks, 100 times with random halves of the features, whether two texts are more alike than either is to a crowd of “impostor” writers. Here the impostors were up to 37 other commenters, and every text was a 3,000-word sample. Known same-author and different-author pairs were run first to calibrate the scale.

Each dot is one comparison and the vertical tick is the mean. Different-author pairs mostly score 0: 53 of 55 fall below Doeland’s mean. Baber’s own YouTube text scores only 0.10–0.30 against his own Facebook and letters, so this method struggles across registers. Doeland actually matches Baber’s Facebook writing (0.45) better than Baber’s YouTube text does.

Baseline: 2 of 55 different-author control pairs scored as high as Doeland–Baber. One of those, a pair of background accounts scoring 1.0, may itself be a shared author or heavy mutual quoting.

Caveat: The score varies a lot between draws (0.01–1.0), and many same-author controls reach 1.0, so this is supportive rather than decisive.

9ModerateMachine learningStylometry

Machine-learning classifier rates Doeland as more “Baber-like” than any other commenter, and more than Baber’s own unseen YouTube text

A logistic-regression model was trained on topic-masked character 2–4-grams plus the 150 most frequent words, using 300-word chunks of Baber’s YouTube, social and correspondence writing versus everyone else. Each background account was scored by a model that never saw that account (leave-one-author-out).

Mean probability that a 300-word chunk is Baber’s. Doeland: 0.64, with 75% of chunks classified as Baber. Without Baber’s YouTube text in training, Doeland still scores 0.57.

Baseline: Highest background account: @kronachwaltz at 0.57.

Caveat: The classifier is only moderately accurate: Baber’s own held-out YouTube text scores 0.59. The margin over the top background accounts is real but small.

2. Professor Fridland’s method and how it was applied

In Killer in the Code Chapter 14, linguist Valerie Fridland compares known and questioned writings across a scorecard of 28 categories. She looks for:

  • Punctuation style, e.g., commas where periods belong; shifts into ALL CAPS.
  • Sentence structure, e.g., run-ons built from independent clauses.
  • Rhetorical questions, and answering questions nobody asked.
  • Modal verbs, especially unusual “shall”.
  • Discourse markers, e.g., a sentence-initial “Yes” that answers nothing.
  • Low-frequency vocabulary, and rare words that occur together.
  • Shared misspellings, which she treats as especially telling.

Every one of these was measured here (§3):

  • Shared with Baber: rhetorical questions, self-answered questions, tag questions, formal connectors, the em-dash family, the ellipsis character (…), “That said,” and the vocative em dash (“Craig—”).
  • Shared rare words and misspellings: “distractors”, “submissible”, “remedial”.
  • Not shared: “shall” (Baber 5, Doeland 0) and the discourse “Yes” (Baber above the background, Doeland not).
  • Not distinctive for anyone: comma splices and ALL-CAPS emphasis are common among all commenters.

One step was added. Fridland’s comparison had no control group, and Baber has argued on YouTube that none is needed (“It does not require a large matched demographic control group before any conclusion can be drawn”). Here every habit is checked against 2,746 people writing about the same cases on the same videos. A shared habit counts only if it is unusual in that population. This makes the comparison stricter than hers, and Doeland still stands far apart from everyone else.

4. What doesn’t match, and other explanations

  • Em-dash intensity. Baber’s YouTube writing has about 18 em dashes per 1,000 words and often ends on a dangling dash (“Thank you—”). Doeland has about 4 per 1,000 words, far above the background median of 0 but well below Baber, and almost never ends on a dash.
  • Doeland-only habits: “mate” (37), “Cheers”, “LoL”, “an A for effort”, “haters” (10 vs Baber’s 1), “simple” used as an adverb (“He simple revisited it”, 3), “suppose to”.
  • Baber-only habits: “of” for “have” (7), “shall” (5), “Do you understand?” (3).
  • Raw habit-rate distance: a Delta-style distance over the habit rates places Doeland mid-pack, because he uses Baber’s habits at different strengths.
  • Other explanations to weigh:
    • Someone close to Baber, writing with his input or on his device. This fits the encoding glitch and the private knowledge as well as Baber himself does, and linguistics alone can’t rule it out.
    • An imitator. This doesn’t explain the device-level glitch, or vocabulary and knowledge that weren’t yet public.
    • AI writing tools used by both. This could account for some em dashes and formal connectors, but not the shared misspellings, the emoji or the encoding artifact.
  • In 14 comments Doeland discusses his own identity. He denies being Baber, and claims to have “interviewed him” twice, to be “a professional in the field of criminology”, and to have a 13-year-old son who solved the Z13. These claims can’t be checked from the text.

5. Tests that didn’t discriminate

  • Garbled-mention glitch (“@T @TM-ew1ig o your own research”, where the next word loses its first letter). Both accounts have it, but so do about two dozen other accounts. It’s a common YouTube app bug.
  • Chunk-by-chunk attribution: 69% (function words) and 85% (characters) of Doeland’s 1,000-word chunks land nearest a Baber register. Some background accounts score as high, because Baber has four profiles in the pool.
  • Sentence-level paraphrase of Baber: 3.6% of Doeland’s sentences closely echo a Baber sentence. Critics who quote Baber score as high or higher (up to 7.7%).
  • Common taunts (“genius”, “bud”, “ya”, “lol”, “so-called”, “due diligence”) are widespread in the background.
  • Who they argue with (overlap of @-mention targets) and comment-removal timing showed nothing unusual.
  • Other suspected accounts, for reference: @nillydilly-w5l shares 6 habits and scores 0.15 on General Impostors; @MartianMan-ate1 shares 5 and scores 0.00. Both corpora are small (under 2,000 words).

6. Data and methods

  • Doeland corpus: all 193 @JackDoeland comments in comments_repo.json (the source of comment_report_JackDoeland.html), including 16 since removed. Lines quoting an opponent without quotation marks were excluded from the vocabulary analysis, such as the critic’s list in his long rebuttal. Quotations of 40+ characters were removed everywhere.
  • Baber corpus: alex-baber-corpus.html, split by register:
    • YouTube @CCCOA.US_2021: 17,000 words.
    • Facebook, X, Threads and Instagram: 34,000.
    • Emails and messages: 13,600.
    • Substack and website: 19,000.
    • Spoken transcripts: 178,000, used for vocabulary only.
    Punctuation and typography measures used only machine-captured text (YouTube, the Facebook scrape, the X archive, PDFs), not screenshot transcriptions.
  • Background population: every other author in comments_repo.json (2,744 accounts, 9,127 comments, about 407,000 words, 147 videos), excluding the four suspected accounts. Heavy accounts with at least 2,000 words (21) were used for scorecard and stylometry baselines.
  • Techniques and parameters:
    • Fridland scorecard: 42 habits, 200 × 2,000-word samples, 75th-percentile rule with leave-one-out.
    • N-gram rarity: background document frequency combined with English Zipf frequency from wordfreq.
    • Cosine Delta on the 150 most frequent words.
    • Character 3–4-grams on topic-masked text (top 300 words kept).
    • General Impostors: 3,000-word samples, 100 iterations, min-max similarity, 25 impostors per iteration, calibrated on 17 same-author and 55 different-author pairs.
    • Logistic regression on masked character 2–4-grams plus the 150 most frequent words, 300-word chunks, leave-one-author-out.
    • Unicode code-point scan for invisible characters.
  • Limitations:
    • Comment dates come from YouTube’s relative labels, accurate to within weeks.
    • The tests are not statistically independent, so their strengths can’t simply be multiplied.
    • The background is topic-matched but modest in size.
    • Baber’s YouTube comments (Aug–Oct 2026) come after most of Doeland’s (Jan–Jun 2026), which helps rule out copying but means the two were written at different times.
  • Builds on: the July 23, 2026 report in can claude find linguistic fingerprints in sockpuppet accounts/, which compared Doeland with Baber’s messages and Substack only, with no population baseline. Visual avatar comparisons in jackdoeland baber avatar comparisons/ were not analysed here.
  • Scripts: the scratchpad auth/ folder (prep, features, scorecard, stylometry, lexical, targets, insider, behavior, report).
