1. Purpose of this analysis ↑ Back to top

This document sets aside the underlying factual disputes covered in the main show notes — whether Marvin Margolis is Zodiac, whether Baber worked as a car salesman, whether the "Elizabeth" sketch says "ZODIAC" — and asks a narrower, different question: when Alex Baber and accounts connected to him respond to critics, do those responses follow a recognizable psychological pattern?

That pattern is DARVO — Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender — a term coined by psychologist Jennifer J. Freyd to describe how people confronted with evidence of wrongdoing often respond: they deny the behavior occurred, attack the credibility and motives of whoever raised it, and then reverse the roles so the confronter appears to be the aggressor and the confronted party appears to be the victim.[1]

This analysis works through five bodies of Baber's own written material — his official YouTube account, four YouTube accounts suspected (with varying strength of evidence) of being operated by him or on his behalf, a Facebook persona very likely connected to him, his private correspondence with Oranchak, and his formal legal correspondence — and catalogs specific statements that fit each stage of the DARVO pattern. Where useful, each example is paired with Freyd's own description of how that stage typically operates, particularly her writing on the "gaslighting" function of the Attack and Reverse stages.[1]

This is a rhetorical-pattern analysis, not a legal or clinical diagnosis, and it does not by itself establish that any specific underlying allegation against Baber is true. Readers should weigh the caveats in the conclusion before drawing broader conclusions.

2. What is DARVO? ↑ Back to top

Jennifer J. Freyd, a psychology professor at the University of Oregon, introduced the term DARVO near the end of a 1997 paper on betrayal trauma theory, describing a pattern she had observed in perpetrators confronted about abusive or wrongful conduct.[1] The acronym breaks into three moves, typically performed in sequence and often blurring together:

  • Deny. The offender denies that the behavior occurred at all, or denies its severity or significance.
  • Attack. The offender goes on the offensive against the person raising the concern — attacking their credibility, competence, motives, or mental state, rather than engaging with the substance of what was raised.
  • Reverse Victim and Offender. The offender recasts themselves as the one being wronged — by a false accusation, a smear campaign, a personal vendetta — while the original victim, whistleblower, or critic is recast as the aggressor.

Subsequent empirical work by Freyd and colleagues found that DARVO responses measurably increase the audience's perception that the true victim is unreliable and that the offender is the more credible party — which is precisely why the tactic is effective regardless of whether the underlying denial is true.[2][3] Freyd and Sarah Harsey have also written specifically about DARVO's overlap with defamation claims and threatened litigation — relevant background given the material in Section 7 below.[4]

Central to this analysis is a passage from Freyd's own writing describing what she calls the gaslighting function of the Attack stage:

"…I have observed that actual abusers threaten, bully and make a nightmare for anyone who holds them accountable or asks them to change their abusive behavior. This attack, intended to chill and terrify, typically includes threats of law suits, overt and covert attacks on the whistle-blower's credibility, and so on. The attack will often take the form of focusing on ridiculing the person who attempts to hold the offender accountable. […] The offender rapidly creates the impression that the abuser is the wronged one, while the victim or concerned observer is the offender. Figure and ground are completely reversed. […] The offender is on the offense and the person attempting to hold the offender accountable is put on the defense." — Jennifer J. Freyd[1]

Section 7 maps this passage, clause by clause, onto Baber's own legal correspondence and messages.

4. Deny ↑ Back to top

The clearest, highest-volume evidence of the Deny stage is Baber's 29-point written rebuttal, submitted under right-of-reply ahead of Part 3's publication. Of 29 numbered allegations, the overwhelming majority are answered with a flat denial — frequently the single word "False" — with little or no engagement with the specific evidence behind the claim.[11]

Right-of-reply rebuttal, item 8

"False and unsupported. I have never created or operated fake accounts in support of myself. The claim that every supporter must be me is baseless speculation. Supporters, family, and friends have been harassed over this accusation…"

A direct, categorical denial of the sockpuppet allegation examined throughout this document — notable because it is answered with the same blanket "False" used for dozens of unrelated claims below, rather than any account-specific rebuttal.[11]

Sockpuppet account — Hummingbirdshum

"Thank you for covering this, it's refreshing to see The Times take the research seriously… Yes, I'm very much real and not a sockpuppet lol 🙏LTST"

A comment denying sockpuppet status appears directly beneath praise for coverage of Baber.[12]

Sockpuppet account — JackDoeland

"@Imnottheborough Unlucky for you, I'm not Alex, Powell. Now run back to your Zodiac group and be a good boy while the men work."

Another identity denial, immediately followed by an Attack-stage insult — illustrating how the Deny and Attack stages typically arrive fused together rather than as separate, discrete moves.[13]

Official account — @CCCOA.US_2021

"Your claim that I deleted my previous comment is false. The Times removed it because of my use of the word 'ignorance'; I did not delete it myself."

A narrow, verifiable-sounding denial on a small point (who removed a comment), used to imply that a critic's broader claims are similarly unreliable.[14]

Right-of-reply rebuttal, item 14

"False. I have never worked in a grocery store produce department. Any background check reaching that conclusion is incorrect or based on false information. Provide background check for verification."

One of many single-word "False" responses; the demand that the accuser "provide" proof reverses the evidentiary burden onto the source rather than Baber. The same denial recurs, nearly verbatim, in Baber's own signed Substack rebuttal three weeks later — "which is absolutely false (though some employment details were partially accurate…)" — showing the line held consistently across a private and a public channel.[11][35]

Right-of-reply rebuttal, item 20

"These are blatant, unsupported lies that are reprehensible. I have zero record of fraud, dishonesty, drug addiction, abuse or violence."

Absence of a formal record is offered as proof of innocence, a common move in Deny-stage rhetoric that does not by itself address the underlying claim.[11]

Right-of-reply rebuttal, item 21

"False. No evidence exists because the claim is fabricated. It appears intended only to damage my reputation."

The denial ("False") is immediately paired with a Reverse-stage framing (the claim exists "only to damage my reputation") — again showing how the three DARVO stages interlock in practice rather than running as separate steps.[11]

Right-of-reply rebuttal, item 24

"This claim is offensive. I was formally diagnosed on the autism spectrum in childhood by qualified medical professionals. Suggesting otherwise is both false and inappropriate."

Compare this categorical claim of formal childhood diagnosis to Baber's own recorded statement on The Missing Podcast, quoted in the main show notes, in which he describes Asperger's only as something "similar to some of my known traits" rather than a diagnosis — and to his private message to Oranchak stating "I never said I had Asperger's."[15]

Private message to Oranchak, July 2026

"One final point: You have claimed that I use or participate in drugs. This is the first time I have ever been accused of such. I have no record of drug use whatsoever, not even as a teenager… To even attempt to make that claim is offensive not only me but to my family."

Note the escalation embedded in the denial itself — from disputing the claim to framing the questioner as having offended Baber's family, a small-scale preview of the Reverse-stage move analyzed at length in Section 6.[9]

Private message to Oranchak, July 2026

"Yes—I was not fired from the position. There were no accusations or reports of theft surrounding my departure. If any such exist, I invite you to share them."

This denial of termination and theft allegations at a former employer directly contradicts a named, on-the-record witness statement from the business owner, and a second independent witness who corroborates her account — both cited in the main show notes.[16]

6. Reverse victim and offender ↑ Back to top

The Reverse stage is the most rhetorically distinctive of the three: rather than simply denying or attacking, it recasts Baber himself — or his family, or his supporters — as the party under attack, and the critic or reporter as the aggressor.

Private message to Oranchak, July 2026

"I wish you had listened. Take care— … I genuinely had a great deal of respect for you before this. My approach was never personal; it's always been about the cases and about the victims. I wish you would've understood that."

Baber positions himself as the wronged party in what had been a professional relationship, casting Oranchak's reporting as a personal betrayal rather than a response to independently gathered allegations.[9]

Private message to Oranchak, July 2026

"Look, David—no matter what I say or share with you at this point, it's clear it will make no difference. You're too invested in your position to retract your statements. Your credibility will take a hit. Again, that was never my intention. We will let others deal with this."

The predicted reputational harm is projected entirely onto Oranchak ("your credibility will take a hit"), even though Baber is the subject of the allegations under discussion.[9]

Private message to Oranchak, July 2026

"David, you even questioned my autism… I would ask you to consider for a moment how it would feel to have someone publicly accuse you of drug use—especially when the allegation is entirely unfounded. How would that feel to you, David? And how might your own family feel seeing such a serious and baseless claim made against you in public?"

A direct rhetorical inversion: an evidentiary question about self-reported diagnoses and characteristics is reframed as an unprovoked personal attack, then redirected onto Oranchak's own family — the same move used in the Attack-stage example in Section 5.[9]

Right-of-reply rebuttal, item 8

"Supporters, family, and friends have been harassed over this accusation when showing any support of findings or investigations."

The sockpuppet allegation itself — the subject of this document — is reframed as an act of harassment committed against Baber's innocent supporters, rather than a claim to be evaluated on its evidence.[11]

Legal threat, June 21, 2026

"…sources upon which you relied on have placed you in a difficult position publicly. As the underlying facts continue to be presented, several of their claims have proven either inaccurate, incomplete, or completely fabricated due to personal grievances with me. I sincerely hope you will take this into consideration before matters progress beyond the point where a simple public correction or retraction is sufficient to address the record."

Baber's sources are recast as the deceivers, and Oranchak as their unwitting, at-risk instrument — while the underlying claims themselves go unaddressed.[26]

Posted in response to a critical comment thread, this recasts organized scrutiny of Baber's business as a "hate campaign" driven by personal animus — performing the Reverse stage in the third person, on Baber's behalf.[6]

"Witch hunt" framing recurs across multiple sockpuppet comments and casts organized inquiry into Baber's claims as inherently illegitimate, regardless of its evidentiary basis.[27]

8. Conclusion ↑ Back to top

Across seven different sources — an acknowledged official account, four accounts suspected of being connected to Baber, a Facebook persona very likely connected to him, a signed public Threads exchange, signed Substack essays, private correspondence, and formal legal letters — a consistent three-part structure recurs: claims are denied categorically rather than addressed point by point; critics are attacked personally, often with mockery of their spelling, mental state, or motives, rather than engaged on the merits; and Baber (or his family, or his supporters) is consistently repositioned as the victim of a campaign, smear, or hate campaign rather than the subject of independently sourced scrutiny. The clearest and most concerning material — explicit reference to a lawsuit paired with a reference to Oranchak's children, and a formal "actual malice" letter from an attorney — sits squarely within the pattern Freyd describes as the gaslighting function of the Attack stage.[1]

An earlier draft of this analysis noted that the official @CCCOA.US_2021 YouTube account looked comparatively restrained, with almost all aggressive Attack-stage material confined to the suspected sockpuppet accounts — raising the possibility that this was simply the behavior of anonymous, deniable supporters rather than Baber himself. Baber's own signed Threads posts and Substack essays complicate that reading considerably. Phrases like "character assassination," "attempted smear campaign," "disgruntled former associates," and a critic's motives explained away as "personal and professional investment" all appear under Baber's own name, not an anonymous account's. The YouTube channel specifically may still be measured by comparison, but the broader claim that Attack- and Reverse-stage rhetoric is limited to unverified, deniable accounts does not hold once his signed public writing is included.

Caveats and counterexamples.

  • Attribution is not proof. The connection between Baber and the four "suspected sockpuppet" YouTube accounts rests on circumstantial evidence — shared vocabulary, insider-level knowledge, timing, and behavioral overlap — not a confirmed technical link (IP address, account records, or an admission). If these accounts are not Baber's, then most of the Attack-stage material in Sections 5–7 reflects the behavior of independent, enthusiastic supporters rather than Baber himself, and the analysis should be read as describing his supporters' rhetoric rather than his own.
  • A DARVO pattern is not a verdict. Identifying DARVO-shaped rhetoric describes how someone responds to an accusation; it does not by itself establish whether the underlying accusation (car-salesman history, theft, abuse, stolen valor, and so on) is true. A person who is genuinely innocent of a false accusation can also respond with denial, anger, and a sense of persecution that superficially resembles this pattern.
  • Not all pushback is empty of substance. Several of the sockpuppet comments quoted or referenced here cite specific documents — grand jury testimony, 1950 census records, USC transcripts — and engage in genuine (if one-sided) argument about the underlying cases. The DARVO framework applies to the rhetorical pattern layered on top of that material (ad hominem redirection, credibility attacks, victim framing), not to the substantive case research itself, which this document does not evaluate.
  • Most sockpuppet-account content is not attack material. The majority of comments collected from the four suspected accounts are enthusiastic but unremarkable case commentary, congratulatory remarks, or emoji reactions to podcast episodes — not hostile exchanges.
